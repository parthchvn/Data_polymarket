# Candidate-human trade filter: methodology and limits

## Purpose

This filter is a **behavioral screen**, not an identity detector. Its purpose is to remove wallets whose observed activity is strongly consistent with systematic arbitrage, high-frequency automation, or market making, leaving a narrower set of **candidate-human trades** for analysis.

`human_candidate` means only: *the wallet was not excluded by the selected rules and the evidence available to this run*. It does **not** mean that a natural person was verified. A person can use an API or a bot, a bot can trade slowly, several people can control one wallet, and one operator can rotate among wallets. No result from this fill-only, pseudonymous dataset resolves those cases.

The method is intentionally auditable: classification is done at wallet level; every exclusion should carry one or more reason codes and the metrics that triggered them; thresholds are configurable; and uncertain wallets remain distinguishable from candidate humans. The criteria below have not been validated against a representative ground-truth set of Polymarket humans and bots, so no accuracy, precision, or recall claim is made.

## What the data can and cannot show

The principal input, `market_decisions.parquet`, contains executed `OrderFilled` events attributed to an order owner. The useful pre-outcome fields include wallet, execution timestamp, maker/taker role, action, outcome token, shares, execution price, market, block number, log index, and, after a fresh extraction with the updated extractor, order hash.

From those fields it is possible to measure:

- observed execution count, distinct active UTC days, and the peak execution count on one UTC day;
- maker/taker participation among **filled** orders;
- trading breadth within the four imported markets;
- buy/sell and equivalent-YES flow balance;
- execution bursts at the timestamp/block resolution of the data;
- repeated fills of the same order when `order_hash` is present; and
- whether observed sells require inventory that is absent from the imported exchange-fill history.

The data cannot establish:

- whether the controller is a natural person, firm, script, or mixed human/API workflow;
- order submission time, cancellation time, quote lifetime, unfilled orders, posted spread, order-to-trade ratio, or continuous two-sided quoting;
- reaction latency to news, a quote, or another venue;
- colocated or latency-minimizing infrastructure, the usual regulatory concept of HFT;
- the identity of one controller across several wallets;
- complete inventory, because transfers, Conditional Token splits/merges, and negative-risk conversions are not in this fill history; or
- venue-wide lifetime activity when only the four selected markets are supplied.

Those are substantive identification limits, not merely missing refinements. MiFID II, for example, defines high-frequency algorithmic trading partly through infrastructure and high intraday **message** rates, while the European Commission's implementing rules count submitted, modified, and cancelled messages. Executed fills alone do not satisfy that definition.[^mifid][^eu-message-rate] ESMA likewise finds that indirect HFT identification methods can produce materially different estimates.[^esma]

## Evidence hierarchy

The filter uses the following evidence in descending order of directness. No single behavioral metric is treated as proof of automation.

### 1. Literature-identified systematic arbitrage wallets

Saguillo et al. detect executed market-rebalancing and combinatorial arbitrage on Polymarket and rank accounts that repeatedly captured those opportunities.[^saguillo] Six addresses from that study occur in the present four-market data and are tagged `literature_identified_systematic_arbitrageur`:

| Address |
|---|
| `0xD218E474776403A330142299f7796E8BA32Eb5C9` |
| `0x63D43bbb87F85AF03b8F2F9E2fAd7B54334fA2f1` |
| `0x9d84cE0306F8551e02EFef1680475Fc0f1dC1344` |
| `0x44c1DfE43260C94Ed4F1D00dE2e1f80Fb113Ebc1` |
| `0xB7d54Bf1D0a362Beb916d9Cb58a04C41D67e0789` |
| `0x53d2D3c78597A78402D4dB455a680dA7EF560c3F` |

Address matching is case-insensitive. The tag records a published behavioral finding, not a verified legal identity and not proof that every trade from the wallet was generated by software. Saguillo et al. describe some large accounts as having “bot-like” bid activity, but their arbitrage evidence can also be consistent with a sophisticated person or human-supervised system. The human-only policy excludes these wallets by default as a documented conservative choice; `--disable-literature-exclusions` switches off that source, and a reviewed manual override has final say. The reason remains separate from model-derived reasons so it can be audited.

These six addresses are reference seeds, not a training set and not a basis for estimating classifier precision.

### 2. Venue-wide activity threshold

Della Vedova's operational screen classifies a wallet as algorithmic when it has **more than 50 trades on its peak active day or more than 1,000 total trades**.[^della-paper][^della-method] The accompanying robustness analysis varies those cutoffs:

| Human-retention profile | Activity exclusion rule | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| strict | `peak_utc_day_trades > 20` or `total_trades > 500` | Removes more possible automation, but also more active humans |
| balanced | `peak_utc_day_trades > 50` or `total_trades > 1,000` | Published baseline |
| lenient | `peak_utc_day_trades > 100` or `total_trades > 2,000` | Retains more wallets, with greater automation-contamination risk |

The peak is the maximum count across the wallet's UTC calendar days, not its average over active days. Threshold equality does not trigger a strict `>` rule. These are sensitivity profiles, not calibrated probabilities or natural boundaries between people and software. A wallet just below a cutoff is not materially different from one just above it.

The rule should be applied to a venue-wide reference history whenever possible. Applying it only to the four markets can create both false negatives and false positives: activity elsewhere is missed, while the peak among locally observed market-days need not represent the wallet's typical venue-wide behavior. Differences in the unit called a “trade” also matter. This repository records executions; another source may reconcile a matched trade differently. Counts should be harmonized before importing published thresholds.

### 3. Fill-side market-making pattern

Polymarket's own market-making guide describes market making as continuously posting bids and asks, quoting both sides, and managing inventory; it also notes that passive GTC/GTD orders and immediately executable FAK/FOK orders can be used in the workflow.[^polymarket-mm][^polymarket-orders] The classical empirical intermediary definition in Kirilenko et al. similarly combines repeated buying and selling with low inventory, but its numeric qualification rules were designed for E-mini futures and are sample-specific; the paper then calls the 16 most active qualified intermediaries HFTs, not a generic fixed percentage of all traders.[^kirilenko]

Accordingly, the filter uses the following only as a **joint supportive pattern**, calculated over a minimum sample and exposed in the audit output:

- high maker-fill share;
- activity on both sides of equivalent binary exposure;
- low observed net-to-gross flow; and
- sustained activity across multiple dates or markets.

For a binary Yes/No market, equivalent-YES signed flow is normalized as follows:

| Fill | Equivalent-YES flow |
|---|---:|
| buy Yes | `+shares` |
| sell Yes | `-shares` |
| buy No | `-shares` |
| sell No | `+shares` |

Useful diagnostics are `abs(sum(flow)) / sum(abs(flow))` for net-to-gross flow and `2 * min(positive_flow, negative_flow) / (positive_flow + negative_flow)` for the filter's zero-to-one two-sided balance. They describe observed executions, not the account's true portfolio.

The implemented strong market-maker footprint requires all of the following within a wallet-market:

- at least five maker-active UTC days;
- maker activity at or above the greater of 150 units and the eligible peer distribution's 90th percentile;
- a one-sided 95% Wilson lower bound of at least 0.85 for maker-fill share;
- two-sided maker-flow balance of at least 0.70;
- at least 20 positive-exposure and 20 negative-exposure maker activity units; and
- distinct maker counterparties at or above the greater of 50 and the eligible peer distribution's 75th percentile.

Maker activity uses distinct non-null `order_hash` values when available. Rows lacking a hash fall back to distinct blocks; an actor-market can consequently report `order_hash`, `mixed_order_hash_block_fallback`, `distinct_block_fallback`, or `no_maker_rows` mode. A review-level market-maker footprint lowers the Wilson bound to 0.75 and requires two of the three supporting conditions (flow balance, both-side activity, counterparty breadth), while retaining the five-day and adaptive activity requirements. The peer quantiles use `interpolation="higher"`; a market uses its own peers when at least 100 eligible actor-markets are available and otherwise uses the global reference pool. These cutoffs are conservative design choices, not published or validated Polymarket ground truth.

#### Why maker/taker alone is wrong

`actor_role == maker` is a property of one fill, not a profession or an identity:

- A human can place a resting limit order and receive a maker fill.
- A market-making algorithm can cross the spread as taker to rebalance inventory or reduce risk.
- An arbitrage algorithm is often predominantly a taker.
- A wallet can switch roles across markets and time.
- This dataset sees only orders that filled, so “maker share” is not quote presence, quote duration, quote competitiveness, cancellation intensity, or a two-sided quoting obligation.

For those reasons the policy classifies and filters whole wallets, not merely their maker rows. Dropping only maker rows would retain many automated taker fills and would split a single strategy into misleading “human” and “non-human” fragments. Conversely, dropping every wallet that ever made liquidity would remove ordinary limit-order users.

### 4. Timing and regularity diagnostics

Niedermayer, Saggese, and Haslhofer find that time, frequency, and transaction-cost features help separate a small labeled set of Ethereum financial bots from humans; examples include transaction frequency, time-of-day entropy, inter-arrival behavior, transactions per block, and long inactivity gaps.[^ethereum-bots] Mappable diagnostics here include inter-block gaps, active-hour coverage, and maximum distinct blocks per hour or day.

These features are supporting evidence only. The published classifier should not be transplanted: its ground truth contains 133 human and 137 bot addresses, several important Ethereum features are unavailable here, and Polymarket's off-chain matching plus relayed on-chain settlement changes the meaning of a transaction timestamp.

The implemented high-frequency-taker footprint is deliberately taker-only and operates on one observation per distinct block, not raw fill rows. A wallet-market first needs at least 200 taker blocks across at least three active days. It is strongly flagged when at least two of these five signals fire:

- taker blocks per active day at or above the eligible peer 95th percentile, with an absolute floor of 30;
- the one-sided 95% Wilson lower bound on the share of successive taker-block gaps no longer than five seconds at or above the peer 95th percentile, with a floor of 0.15;
- maximum taker blocks in one hour at or above the peer 95th percentile, with a floor of 50;
- maximum taker blocks in one day at or above the peer 95th percentile, with a floor of 100; or
- activity in at least 12 distinct UTC hours on at least two days.

Exactly one signal produces `review`, not an exclusion. The same market/global peer-pool fallback described above is used. This is a high-frequency **execution footprint**, not proof of regulatory HFT or measured reaction latency.

## Exact execution-data caveats

### Order hashes and fill fragmentation

The legacy selected fill artifact was extracted by a version of `xvi_extract.py` that deliberately skipped `order_hash`. In that artifact, `(block_number, log_index)` uniquely identifies an event, but it does **not** reveal that several events are partial executions of one submitted order. A single resting maker order can therefore contribute several decision rows and can look more active or bursty than the order-placement behavior warrants.

The updated extractor retains `order_hash`; it takes effect only after `python xvi_extract.py fills` and downstream scoring are rerun. Existing Parquet outputs are not repaired merely because the code changed. When the column is present, the filter reports hash coverage and uses distinct order hashes as maker activity units; missing-hash maker rows use a distinct-block fallback. Executions remain separate rows in the classified outputs because each fill remains economically real.

Even with `order_hash`, the archive is fill-only: it contains no never-filled orders and no cancellations. For a maker order, the first observed fill is only an **upper bound** on placement time. Order hash therefore fixes one fragmentation problem but does not make quote lifetime, cancellation rate, or order-to-trade analysis possible.

Multiple maker events can also be matched by one taker order in a single transaction. Same-second, same-block, or adjacent-log events are not necessarily independent decisions. The filter therefore uses order-hash-aware maker activity with a block fallback and distinct-block-only taker timing. The published peak-day/total-activity screen still counts distinct decision IDs, so it can remain inflated by partial fills of one resting order; its reason code and this limitation must remain visible.

### Taker timing

The event timestamp is a Polygon block/settlement timestamp. Polymarket documents that an order may match before its trade settles on-chain, and marketable orders can also be delayed by a market-specific matching delay.[^polymarket-quickstart][^polymarket-orders] Thus a taker timestamp shows when an execution was recorded on chain, not when the trader observed information, signed the order, submitted it to the CLOB, or received the match response. Relaying, batching, block production, and the dataset's timestamp resolution all confound apparent latency.

Taker inter-block and burst metrics can describe execution intensity. They cannot establish sub-second HFT, reaction time, or a latency advantage. `block_number` and `log_index` provide deterministic execution ordering, not wall-clock decision timing. Maker timestamps are never used as reaction-speed evidence because a maker order may have rested before it filled.

### Incomplete inventory

`external_inventory_detected` is raised elsewhere in the scoring pipeline when cumulative observed sells in a market exceed observed buys. It is evidence that holdings entered through transfers, splits, merges, negative-risk conversions, a pre-window position, or other history absent from the imported fills. In that case, apparent inventory neutrality, turnover, holding time, and round trips are not reliable enough for a hard market-maker exclusion.

The inverse does not hold: `external_inventory_detected == false` does not prove complete inventory. Missing operations may offset, and history can be left-censored without producing a negative running balance. The implemented classifier therefore does not use `external_inventory_detected`, reconstructed position, holding time, turnover, or P&L as a direct label input. Its observed maker-flow balance is one condition in a larger joint pattern and must not be described as true inventory neutrality.

## No profit or outcome leakage

Automation classification must not use settlement, the winning outcome, realized payoff, profit, return, win rate, forecast correctness, or any feature calculated from information unavailable at execution. In this repository that excludes, among other fields, `settlement_payout`, `decision_payoff_usd`, `decision_payoff_after_fee_usd`, and any resolved-label correctness measure.

There are two reasons:

1. Profitability and correctness measure skill, luck, market exposure, and execution quality, not whether a human clicked the order.
2. Using future resolution information would leak the target markets' outcomes into a filtered dataset intended for behavioral or performance analysis.

The classifier's explicit feature boundary is `decision_id`, wallet, market, maker/taker role, timestamp, block, log index, action, outcome token, shares, counterparty wallet, and optional order hash. Execution price and all settlement/payoff columns can be carried into an output row but do not affect its label. Outcomes may be analyzed **after** the wallet labels are frozen, but must never determine those labels. Likewise, the literature seed above is tagged from a direct arbitrage-pattern study rather than reconstructed from local P&L.

## Venue-wide reference history

The four selected markets are a left-censored view of each wallet. The preferred production workflow is to build wallet features from venue-wide `OrderFilled` history through at least the maximum timestamp in the target data, using the same event normalization and, where available, `order_hash`. Freeze and record the source revision, observation window, row-count convention, and file hash so classifications are reproducible.

A useful supplemental reference is the Akey et al. academic Polymarket dataset and its `user_features` table, which includes `n_trades`, `active_days`, maker shares, market breadth/concentration, inter-arrival, regularity, burst, both-sides, and round-trip features.[^akey-paper][^akey-data] It covers 2022-11-11 through 2026-03-29. It is consequently incomplete for target executions after 2026-03-29 and should be versioned, date-checked, and treated as supplemental rather than silently assumed current. The current `--reference-decisions` option expects normalized decision-level rows with the classifier's feature boundary; it does not directly accept Akey's aggregated `user_features` file without a separate adapter.

Venue-wide enrichment should join lower-cased **trading wallet** addresses. Polymarket can use proxy/smart-wallet structures, so a trading address must not be assumed to equal a person's signer or to identify one natural person.[^polymarket-wallets]

If no suitable reference history is supplied, output must say that features are local to four markets. Low local activity is then weak absence-of-evidence, not positive evidence of humanity. The default high-purity analysis should use `human_candidate_decisions.parquet` and omit `uncertain_decisions.parquet`, which combines `review` and `insufficient_history` rows.

## Interpreting filter outputs

Wallet-level labels should be read as policy buckets:

| Label | Meaning | Human-only default |
|---|---|---|
| `likely_automated_or_mm` | A literature-address, activity, strong market-maker, or strong high-frequency-taker exclusion fired | Written to `excluded_decisions.parquet` |
| `review` | One review-level behavioral footprint fired without a stronger exclusion | Written to `uncertain_decisions.parquet` |
| `insufficient_history` | Fewer than 20 distinct observed blocks and no stronger signal | Written to `uncertain_decisions.parquet` |
| `human_candidate` | No implemented screen fired and at least 20 distinct blocks were observed | Written to `human_candidate_decisions.parquet`; never call “verified human” |

One wallet can have several reason codes even if the exported policy label is singular. The audit table should preserve the literature flag, activity counts and source window, maker shares, two-sided and net-to-gross diagnostics, timing diagnostics, order-hash coverage, fill fragmentation, inventory-coverage flag, chosen profile, and final include/exclude decision.

The Saguillo tag is a reason code within `likely_automated_or_mm`, not a fifth wallet label. The row field is `row_classification`, the wallet field is `wallet_classification`, and the wallet-market field is `actor_market_classification`. The complete outputs are:

- `classified_decisions.parquet`;
- `human_candidate_decisions.parquet`;
- `uncertain_decisions.parquet`;
- `excluded_decisions.parquet`;
- `actor_market_features.parquet`;
- `wallet_features.parquet`;
- `wallet_classification.csv`;
- `thresholds.json`; and
- `classification_manifest.json`.

Reason codes identify the actual path to a label: strong or review market-maker footprint, strong or review high-frequency-taker footprint, the selected published activity screen, literature identification, insufficient blocks, no strong evidence, or a documented manual override. One wallet can carry more than one reason. The default `--scope wallet` applies its wallet label to all of its rows, which is the appropriate human-only policy. `--scope market` localizes behavioral footprints to a wallet-market, but literature identification, the published activity screen, and manual overrides remain wallet-global.

Results should be reported under more than one threshold profile. Stability across strict, balanced, and lenient settings is more informative than a single hard-filter count. Downstream estimates should state which profile was used, whether uncertain rows were removed, whether venue-wide history was available, and what share of wallets, fills, and notional was excluded.
